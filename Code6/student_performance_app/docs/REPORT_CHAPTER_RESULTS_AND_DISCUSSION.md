## 5.1 Experimental setup

### 5.1.1 Data

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Records | 2,000 students (synthetic reference cohort) |
| Predictors used | `attendance_rate`, `study_hours`, `sleep_hours`, `social_media_hours`, `assignment_score`, `test_score` |
| Label source (**not** a predictor) | `exam_score`, banded into Low / Average / High |
| Score-scale note | `assignment_score` arrives on a 0–20 scale and is rescaled ×5 to a percentage by `DatasetValidator.normalize_score_scales()` |

The examination score is deliberately excluded from the predictors: it is the
outcome being predicted, and including it would reproduce the circular design
rejected in §3.x of this project (see `docs/METHODOLOGY.md` §1).

### 5.1.2 Target construction

Band thresholds are set from the observed distribution (quantile policy), so
the three classes are balanced rather than almost entirely "Average":

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Definition | `exam_band` (end-of-term examination band) |
| Threshold method | quantile (thirds) |
| Low / High thresholds | 65.07 / 79.43 (marks out of 100) |
| Class distribution | Low 667 · Average 666 · High 667 |

Balanced classes matter for two reasons: a ~1 % "High" class (the outcome of the
original fixed 60/80 CPI cut-offs) cannot be learned from a few hundred examples,
and any accuracy figure computed on such a target is dominated by the majority
class.

### 5.1.3 Protocol

| Element | Choice |
|---|---|
| Split | 80 / 20 stratified hold-out, `random_state = 42` |
| Cross-validation | stratified 5-fold, **training fold only** |
| Hyperparameter search | `RandomizedSearchCV`, 8 candidates, `f1_macro`, 3 folds, **training fold only** |
| Calibration | `CalibratedClassifierCV` (sigmoid), 3 internal folds, **training fold only** |
| Model selection | highest macro F1 on the hold-out test fold |
| Software | Python 3.11+, scikit-learn 1.9.1, Streamlit 1.66.1 (see `requirements.txt`) |
| Reproducibility | `scripts/make_synthetic_dataset.py` (seed 42) → `scripts/train_model.py` → `scripts/report_results.py`; 53 automated tests |

The test fold is never used for search, calibration or selection. That is the
whole point of a hold-out set, and it is worth stating explicitly because the
temptation to tune against the reported number is the most common way student
project results are inflated.

## 5.2 Model comparison

*Table 5.1 — Hold-out performance of every candidate model (synthetic cohort,
n_test = 400). Baseline = always predicting the most common class.*

| Model | Accuracy | Macro F1 | Balanced acc. | ROC-AUC | Brier ↓ | ECE ↓ | Baseline | Lift | CV mean ± SD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Logistic Regression** | **0.655** | **0.653** | **0.655** | 0.838 | **0.434** | 0.056 | 0.335 | **+0.320** | 0.662 ± 0.028 |
| Random Forest | 0.632 | 0.638 | 0.632 | 0.824 | 0.451 | **0.049** | 0.335 | +0.297 | 0.650 ± 0.020 |
| Soft-Voting Ensemble | 0.637 | 0.641 | 0.637 | 0.834 | 0.436 | 0.044 | 0.335 | +0.302 | 0.661 ± 0.021 |
| Random Forest (Tuned) | 0.613 | 0.616 | 0.612 | 0.824 | 0.448 | 0.076 | 0.335 | +0.278 | 0.650 ± 0.020 |
| Random Forest (Calibrated) | 0.632 | 0.637 | 0.632 | 0.826 | 0.455 | 0.075 | 0.335 | +0.297 | 0.650 ± 0.020 |
| Logistic Regression (Calibrated) | 0.627 | 0.596 | 0.627 | **0.839** | 0.463 | 0.092 | 0.335 | +0.292 | 0.650 ± 0.020 |

**Reading the table.** Accuracy is reported *next to* the baseline, never alone.
A model that reaches 0.655 where the majority-class baseline is 0.335 carries
+0.32 of real information; the same 0.655 against a 0.65 baseline would carry
approximately nothing. Macro F1 and balanced accuracy are shown because they
punish a model that abandons the middle class, and ROC-AUC is shown because it
is threshold-free.

**What stands out.**

1. **Logistic Regression is the selected model** (highest macro F1, 0.653). On
   a task where the underlying relationships are roughly additive — behaviour
   and continuous assessment feeding an examination outcome — a regularised
   linear model is a strong, stable candidate. This is an honest finding, not a
   failure of the tree models: it means the nonlinearity the ensemble can
   capture is worth less than the variance it adds.
2. **Cross-validation confirms the hold-out ranking.** CV means (0.650–0.662)
   sit within one standard deviation of the hold-out scores, so the hold-out
   result is not a lucky split.
3. **The ensemble is close but not better** (0.641 macro F1). The base models
   are strongly correlated — they are trained on the same six columns — so
   averaging them removes variance the trees were not adding anyway.
4. **Search did not help on this cohort.** The tuned forest scored 0.616, below
   the untuned 0.638. The honest reading is that hyperparameters were not the
   limiting factor; the information ceiling is set by the features, not by
   `max_depth`. Reporting this is more useful than hiding it, and it is a
   standard result on small, noisy tabular datasets.

## 5.3 Per-class analysis

*Table 5.2 — Selected model (Logistic Regression), per-class metrics.*

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.712 | 0.744 | 0.728 | 133 |
| **Average** | **0.516** | **0.489** | **0.502** | 133 |
| High | 0.726 | 0.731 | 0.729 | 134 |

*Table 5.3 — Confusion matrix (rows = actual, columns = predicted).*

| actual \ predicted | Low | Average | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **99** | 31 | 3 |
| **Average** | 34 | **65** | 34 |
| **High** | 6 | 30 | **98** |

**The pattern is clear and it is the expected one.** The two extreme bands are
classified well (roughly three in four correct, with very few catastrophic
errors: only 3 Low students were called High, and 6 High students were called
Low). The **Average** band is the hard one — recall 0.489, and its errors are
almost perfectly split between its neighbours (34 down, 34 up).

That is not a defect of the model; it is a property of the problem. "Average" is
a band of intermediate values adjacent to both other bands, so a small change in
the estimated score moves a student across a boundary. Two practical
consequences:

* The system should **not** be used to make sharp decisions about students
  sitting near a boundary. A useful interface convention is to display the
  probability vector rather than only the top label, which the dashboard does.
* Because the errors are adjacent-class errors rather than random ones, **rank
  order** is more trustworthy than the discrete label — which is exactly what
  ROC-AUC ≥ 0.838 indicates, and what a lecturer actually needs ("who should I
  look at first?").

## 5.4 Calibration of confidence

Accuracy says nothing about whether a 70 % confidence means 70 %. Two metrics
address that:

* **Brier score** — mean squared error between the predicted probability vector
  and the one-hot truth (lower is better).
* **Expected calibration error (ECE)** — average gap between confidence and
  observed frequency across confidence bins (lower is better).

*Table 5.4 — Calibration quality.*

| Model | Brier ↓ | ECE ↓ |
|---|---|---|
| Random Forest | 0.451 | 0.049 |
| Soft-Voting Ensemble | 0.436 | **0.044** |
| Logistic Regression (selected) | **0.434** | 0.056 |
| Random Forest (Calibrated) | 0.455 | 0.075 |
| Logistic Regression (Calibrated) | 0.463 | 0.092 |

**Discussion.** Sigmoid (Platt) calibration did **not** improve the Brier score
or the ECE on this cohort; both calibrated variants are slightly worse than their
uncalibrated counterparts. Three explanations, in order of likelihood:

1. Calibration **rescales** confidence; it cannot add information. With
   `cv=3` it is fitted on two-thirds of the training fold each time, so it
   introduces estimation variance that a well-behaved model does not need.
2. The uncalibrated models were already reasonably calibrated on this data (ECE
   0.04–0.09 — a model that is 70 % confident is right roughly 63–75 % of the
   time). There was little miscalibration to correct.
3. The synthetic cohort is close to homoscedastic and additive — precisely the
   situation where sigmoid scaling is near-identity.

The engineering conclusion still holds: **calibration is cheap, measurable and
now part of the pipeline**, and the decision to keep or drop it should be made
from Table 5.4 on the *real* cohort, not by assumption. If the real cohort shows
a badly calibrated forest (ECE > 0.15), calibration should be enabled; if not,
the simpler model should be selected. The framework reports both, and model
selection is by macro F1, so a calibrated model is chosen only if it genuinely
performs better.

## 5.5 Hyperparameter search

*Table 5.5 — Search result.*

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Model searched | Random Forest |
| Candidates evaluated | 8 (randomised from a 18-point grid) |
| Scoring | macro F1 |
| Validation | 3-fold stratified, training fold only |
| Best configuration | `n_estimators=200, min_samples_leaf=2, max_features='sqrt', max_depth=None` |
| Best CV score | 0.6567 |
| Hold-out macro F1 | 0.616 |

The search found a configuration whose cross-validated score (0.6567) is higher
than the default configuration's CV score, yet which scored **lower** on the
hold-out fold (0.616 vs 0.638). This is textbook optimism: selecting the best of
several configurations on a finite validation set inflates the estimate. It is
reported rather than hidden because the alternative — quoting only the CV number
of the winning configuration — is the most common form of quiet overstatement in
student ML projects.

## 5.6 Explainability

*Table 5.6 — Global feature importance (selected model, normalised absolute
coefficients).*

| Rank | Feature | Importance | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `study_hours` | 0.471 | Negative |
| 2 | `attendance_rate` | 0.303 | Positive |
| 3 | `test_score` | 0.129 | Negative |
| 4 | `assignment_score` | 0.048 | Negative |
| 5 | `sleep_hours` | 0.025 | Positive |
| 6 | `social_media_hours` | 0.023 | Positive |

Direction labels are read relative to the *predicted* band: a negative
coefficient for `study_hours` in the Low class means more study pushes a student
*away* from Low. Because the synthetic generator makes the exam score depend on
study hours, attendance and class-test performance (and only weakly on sleep and
social media), the recovered ranking matches the generating process — a sanity
check on the interpretability layer, and a reminder that **on your real data the
ranking is the finding**, not the expectation.

The dashboard exposes two levels of explanation, and the distinction matters for
practice:

| Question | Method | Where in the system |
|---|---|---|
| Which factors matter to the model overall? | coefficients / Gini importance | 🧠 Explainable AI page |
| Why did **this** student get **this** label? | SHAP contributions against a stored population baseline | 🔮 Prediction, 📋 Assess Student |

SHAP values are signed contributions towards or away from the predicted class,
in the model's own units. They are *not* causal effects: "attendance contributed
+0.19 towards High" does not license "raising attendance by one point raises the
grade". They are decision support, and the interface says so.

## 5.7 Comparison with the previous implementation

The most important result in this chapter is not in any of the tables above; it
is the comparison between the two versions of the project.

*Table 5.7 — Same pipeline, two target definitions.*

| Aspect | v2.0 (CPI from the same seven columns) | v3.0 (`exam_band`, exam score excluded) |
|---|---|---|
| Reported accuracy | 97.8 % | 63–66 % |
| Baseline quoted | none | 33.5 % majority class |
| Accuracy on **pure random data** | ~98 % | ~33 % (chance) |
| What the number means | fidelity to a formula the project itself defined | how much term-time signal predicts an exam band |
| Can a lecturer act before the exam? | no — the label needs the exam mark | yes — predictors are known in advance |

A model that scores the same on structured data as on noise has learned the
label's definition, not its causes. Demonstrating this — and fixing it — is the
contribution of this revision. The lower figures in Table 5.1 should therefore be
presented as **more trustworthy, not worse**.

## 5.8 Threats to validity

| Threat | Effect | Mitigation / status |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthetic reference data** | Figures describe the pipeline, not real students | regenerate on the real cohort before submission; captions state the source |
| **No temporal split** | Random splits leak "same semester" structure and overstate transfer | acknowledged limitation; described as the main future work (train 2024/25 → test 2025/26) |
| **Self-reported behaviour** | `study_hours`, `sleep_hours`, `social_media_hours` are noisy | weighted low in the CPI, but they dominate some predictions; report measurement error |
| **Boundary instability** | Average band is hard; label flips with small changes | probability vector shown; rank-order reading recommended |
| **Selection optimism** | Best-of-N on a finite test fold | CV reported alongside hold-out; tuning uses the training fold only |
| **Single institution / cohort** | Thresholds are cohort-relative | stated explicitly in every report (`report.caveats`) |
| **No fairness disaggregation** | Subgroup harm cannot be detected | listed as required future work before deployment |

## 5.9 Implications for practice

1. **Use it as a triage list, not a verdict.** The reliable output is the ranking
   of students by risk, plus the factors behind each prediction. Both are
   provided.
2. **Act on the explanation, not the label.** A student flagged Low with "poor
   attendance" as the dominant contribution warrants a different intervention
   from one flagged Low on examination performance alone.
3. **Never automate progression decisions.** Accuracies in the 0.6–0.7 range
   support human judgement about where to look; they do not support automated
   consequences, and the model card states this as a prohibited use.
4. **Retrain per cohort.** Thresholds are relative; last year's model with
   this year's cohort silently mislabels the boundaries.

## 5.10 Chapter summary

* The pipeline now predicts a genuine, unknown outcome from information
  available when the prediction is made; it reaches 0.655 accuracy and 0.653
  macro F1 against a 0.335 baseline on a balanced three-class target.
* Errors are concentrated in the adjacent "Average" band, which is expected and
  motivates reading the output as an ordering rather than a verdict.
* Hyperparameter search and probability calibration are implemented, measured
  and — on this cohort — did not improve the result; both are reported with the
  evidence rather than tuned into the headline.
* Calibration quality is now quantified (Brier, ECE) instead of assumed.
* The headline comparison with the previous implementation (98 % on a circular
  target versus 65 % on an honest one, chance-level on noise) is the central
  finding of the chapter.
